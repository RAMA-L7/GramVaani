# Telugu Voice Technology Research
_Agent 5 | Access date: 2026-11-16 | Status: research notes, no application changes_

## 1. Scope and method

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] Scope: evidence for technical decisions in a Telugu-first voice-assisted reporting **research prototype** for Anganwadi workers in Andhra Pradesh. Planned stack (not built): Next.js frontend, FastAPI backend, pluggable `ASRProvider` and `ExtractionEngine` interfaces, config-driven fields in JSON, SQLite local-first storage, synthetic data only, no assumed cloud dependency. This document records evidence only; no code, config, README, or other research file was created or modified.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] Method: the `websearch` tool returned HTTP 402/429 on every attempt (2026-11-16), so discovery was done by (a) the arXiv Atom API (`export.arxiv.org/api/query`) with targeted `all:` queries, and (b) direct `webfetch` of primary sources: arXiv abstract/HTML pages, official vendor documentation (Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, AWS, Sarvam, MDN, ONNX Runtime, OpenAI/ggml, AI4Bharat), and project repositories. Access date for every source below is 2026-11-16. No credentials or private accounts were used.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] Label semantics used throughout: `[VERIFIED]` = two or more independent credible sources, both listed; `[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE]` = one credible paper or official document, author/publisher named; `[UNVERIFIED]` = vendor marketing, inference, or untested assumption; `[UNKNOWN]` = searched for but not established. Where a number comes from a vendor rather than an independent evaluation, that is stated in the finding itself.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] Two research products surfaced that are unusually close to this project's setting: a Gram Vaani **Hindi** telephonic corpus study of the "reality gap" (Chauhan et al., 2026) and an agricultural-domain ASR benchmark covering **Telugu** (Chandrashekar et al., 2026; Singh et al., 2026). Neither is a Telugu Anganwadi dataset; they are methodological proxies, not direct evidence.

[VERIFIED] **Which options are credible on evidence, not hype.** IndicWhisper has the best independently published Telugu numbers on five heterogeneous benchmarks (Bhogale et al. 2023) and is open-source; it also beat Google STT on 57 of 59 Vistaar benchmarks overall and IndicWav2Vec on 45 of 59. Azure is competitive on read speech (Telugu avg 31.4) but collapses on studio IndicTTS (61.1), which is a warning about single-benchmark comparisons. Sarvam is the only vendor that documents an explicit `codemix` output mode, keyterm prompting, 8 kHz telephony optimisation, and transparent per-minute pricing, but its accuracy claims are vendor-reported.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Benchmark diversity matters more than a leaderboard.** Bhogale et al. report pairwise Spearman correlations of WER rankings across benchmarks, including **−0.26 between Common Voice and IndicTTS**: ranking on one benchmark is "not informative" of ranking on another. Telugu is absent from Common Voice in Vistaar, so any Telugu leaderboard built from that subset alone would be especially fragile.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Historical cost figures are stale.** The Google/Azure per-hour prices above come from the 2023 Vistaar paper; they are included for comparability with the 2023 WERs, not as current quotes. Current pricing was verified only for Sarvam (₹0.49/min, 2026-11-16).

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **IndicTrans2 and IndicXlit are adjacent ecosystem pieces, not ASR.** IndicTrans2 (Gala et al., 2023) is an MIT-licensed NMT family for all 22 scheduled languages with BPCC training data (~230 M bitext pairs; mined corpora CC0, new seed corpora CC-BY-4.0, IN22 test set CC-BY-4.0). IndicXlit (Madhani et al., 2022) is an MIT-licensed ~11 M-parameter transliteration model for 21 Indic languages trained on Aksharantar (26 M word pairs); Telugu Top-1 accuracies: Dakshina 73.38, Aksharantar native words 84.69, named entities 51.82. These are useful for synthetic-data generation and for normalising Roman/Telugu mixing, not for speech recognition.

## 3. Code-mixed Telugu–English speech

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **CoSTA** (Shankar, Jyothi & Bhattacharyya, 2024) is the clearest published code-switched **speech** benchmark involving Telugu: it releases an evaluation set for Bengali-English, Hindi-English, Marathi-English and **Telugu-English** speech → English text, and its architecture fuses ASR text with audio and feeds a pretrained MT module, trained on synthetically created ST data. It reports up to +3.5 BLEU over competitive cascaded and end-to-end baselines. This is speech *translation*, not Telugu-script transcription.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **MUCS 2021** (Diwan et al., Interspeech 2021) provided ~600 h across seven Indian languages including Telugu, but its two **code-switched** pairs were Hindi-English and Bengali-English only. Telugu participated in the *multilingual* track. Baseline WERs were 30.73% (multilingual) and 32.45% (code-switching). Therefore MUCS does **not** supply a Telugu-English code-switched ASR benchmark.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Language-ID work exists but is not recognition.** Krishna D. N. (2020) reports multimodal utterance-level code-switch detection on Microsoft's Telugu-English, Tamil-English and Gujarati-English datasets; Rangan, Teki & Misra (2020) report 3–5% relative LID accuracy gains on Telugu/Tamil/Gujarati code-mixed with English. These detect *whether* a switch occurred; they do not transcribe it.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Entity-dense code-mix is a known hard failure.** Menta (2026) built a held-out synthetic entity-dense Telugu test set (digits, currency, addresses, brand names, English/Indic codemix): `vasista22/whisper-telugu-large-v2` achieved Entity-Hit-Rate 0.027 and Deepgram Nova-3 0.16. A LoRA fine-tune using a ~22,000-utterance TTS↔STT "flywheel" corpus reached EHR 0.473; a native-human sanity check (n=20 Telugu) gave 0.516. All three beta models still missed pre-registered EHR targets (0.75 Telugu). This is a single-author paper and its cost figure (<$50 marginal) is author-reported.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Script collapse is Telugu-specific in that study.** Menta (2026) reports that vanilla Whisper-large-v3 exhibits Telugu Script-Collapse Rate 0.46–0.71, corrected by a per-language LoRA to 0.81–0.97, and explicitly warns the recipe is contraindicated on Hindi and Tamil (where vanilla SFR ≥ 0.98).

[UNVERIFIED] **Sarvam Saaras says it supports "code-mixed audio" and offers a `codemix` mode** that keeps English words in Latin script and Indic words in native script. The documentation gives an example in Hindi, not Telugu, and gives no Telugu WER/EHR. Treat as a promising capability to test, not established accuracy.

[UNKNOWN] **No system was found that is documented as handling spontaneous Telugu–English code-mixed field speech well.** Searches of arXiv (code-mixed/code-switched + Telugu), vendor docs and benchmark papers returned text-only NLP tasks (POS, hate speech, stress, language identification), translation benchmarks, or vendor claims. There is no publicly documented Telugu-English code-switched ASR evaluation on rural/field/handheld recordings.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Observed failure-mode clusters to test for:** (1) script collapse / wrong-script output (Menta 2026); (2) language-ID errors at switches (Krishna 2020; Rangan et al. 2020); (3) loss of named entities, numbers and currency (Menta 2026; Sarvam documents "entity preservation" as a differentiator, which is a vendor claim); (4) academic-name and scheme-name transliteration ambiguity — Motwani/Dakshina-style transliteration accuracy for Telugu named entities is only 51.82 Top-1 (Madhani et al. 2022); (5) domain vocabulary mismatch, which Chauhan et al. (2026) show is primarily an *acoustic* mismatch not fixable by LMs alone.

## 4. Accents, noise and dataset coverage

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Controlled noise: Kathbath-Hard.** Bhogale et al. (2023) added ESC background clips to Kathbath audio at random SNR 3–30 dB. For Telugu, WER rose 25.0 → 27.8 (IndicWhisper), 26.9 → 31.2 (IndicWav2Vec) and 34.3 → 35.9 (Google STT). This is a controlled, repeatable robustness test that can be reproduced in a bake-off.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Regional/district coverage is real but not Anganwadi-specific.** IndicVoices (Javed et al., 2024) covers 145 districts, 16,237 speakers and 22 languages, with 74% extempore and 17% conversational speech; the project README (accessed 2026-11-16) reports a larger release of 12,000 h, 22,563 speakers and 208 districts with 3,200 h transcribed. The paper and README disagree on totals; both are reproduced here rather than reconciled.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **The closest domain analogue is Hindi, not Telugu.** The Gram Vaani ASR challenge (Bhanushali et al., Interspeech 2022) targets spontaneous **Hindi** telephone recordings with regional/dialectal variation. Chauhan et al. (2026, "Navigating the Reality Gap") use that corpus and report IndicWav2Vec degrading from **11.60% WER on clean read Hindi to 41.72% WER on real telephonic Gram Vaani audio**; they evaluate full fine-tuning, LoRA and on-device stream-based continual adaptation where raw audio never leaves the device, with multi-domain experience replay improving target WER by 18.2% relative and reducing catastrophic forgetting by 54%.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Agricultural field recordings have been benchmarked in Telugu.** Chandrashekar et al. (2026) evaluated 10,934 recordings across Hindi, Telugu and Odia with up to 10 ASR models each; Hindi best WER 16.2%, Odia best 35.1%, and speaker diarization with best-speaker selection reduced WER by up to 66% on multi-speaker audio. Singh et al. (2026, "FarmerChat") report a model-agnostic pipeline (gated audio enhancement, diarization/target-speaker selection, ASR, weighted agricultural lexicon correction, quality gate) reducing WER 16–23% relative on cloud ASR and ~5% on an on-device model, with no ASR fine-tuning.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Clinical-domain Telugu evidence exists but is narrow.** Devarakonda et al. (2026) report Telugu medical-domain WER falling from 24.3% to 15.8% using confidence-aware weighting of real + TTS synthetic speech plus a 5-gram KenLM; Kannada fell 31.7% → 25.4%. This supports synthetic-data augmentation with a quantifiable read-prose regression check. SamaVaani (Kumar et al., 2026) audits eight ASR systems (IndicWhisper, Whisper-large-v3, Sarvam, GoogleS2T, Gemma3n, OmniLingual, Vaani, Gemini) on clinical speech, but in Kannada, Hindi and Indian English — **not Telugu**.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Shrutilipi improves noise robustness.** Bhogale et al. (2022) report an average 5.8% WER reduction on IndicSUPERB for seven languages when adding Shrutilipi, plus a 2.3% WER drop for a 10× smaller Conformer, and explicitly demonstrate better robustness to noisy input; Telugu contributes 390 h to Vistaar-Train.

[UNKNOWN] **No published study measures ASR on Telugu speech from rural Anganwadi workers, Telugu regional accents (e.g. Rayalaseema vs Coastal Andhra variations), or handheld field recordings in anganwadi settings.** Searches returned Hindi Gram Vaani, general IndicVoices district coverage, agricultural and clinical Telugu studies, but nothing matching this population or accent set. Accent robustness for this exact deployment is an open experiment.

## 6. Structured extraction, grounding and clarification techniques

[VERIFIED] **Schema-constrained generation fixes structure, not meaning.** Chavan (2026) benchmarked five small LLMs across 14 structured-output tasks under native decoding, Outlines and XGrammar: constrained decoding raised schema validity from 78.6–92.9% to 100% but left a scale-dependent *semantic* gap (type coercions rescued; multi-step instruction-semantic failures not). Independently, Li (2026, "When JSON Is Not Enough") reports 100% schema validity in the strongest model but ~80% semantic success on 2,400 ordering-agent calls, with double-digit schema-valid unsafe acceptances in weaker models. Conclusion: schema conformance is a necessary interface layer, not a substitute for field-level verification.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Outlines** (Willard & Louf, 2023; Apache-2.0; repo `dottxt-ai/outlines`) provides guided generation from Pydantic models, JSON Schema, regex and CFG across OpenAI, Gemini, vLLM, Ollama, transformers and llama.cpp. Its own README documents a "parse event details with incomplete data" example that uses union types to return "I don't know" — directly reusable as a missing-field fallback for config-driven JSON fields.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **SafePassage** (Barrow et al., 2025) operationalises a "safe passage": the LLM extracts entities plus their supporting context, a string-based global aligner checks that context against the source, and a scoring model judges consistency. The three parts together reduced hallucinations by up to 85% on IE tasks. A small encoder fine-tuned on 1–2 h of annotations outperformed an LLM scorer at flagging unsafe passages — relevant for a local-first prototype with no cloud LLM.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **LMDX** (Perot et al., 2023/2024) reframes document IE so extracted entities are localised in the source, giving grounding guarantees. The analogue for speech is forcing every field value to carry the transcript span (word/character offsets) it came from; Whisper/whisper.cpp can supply token-level timestamps, though word-level timestamps are not available for all languages or all providers (e.g. Sarvam explicitly does not support them).

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Multi-stage validation with human review** (Mahbub et al., 2026): prompt calibration → rule-based plausibility filtering → semantic grounding assessment → independent higher-capacity judge LLM → selective expert review → external predictive-validity analysis. Applied to 919,783 clinical notes, rule-based filtering and semantic grounding removed 14.59% of LLM-positive extractions as unsupported/irrelevant/implausible; judge–expert agreement Gwet's AC1 = 0.80. This is the strongest directly transferable pattern for preventing hallucinated field values.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Conformal / calibrated abstention** (Yadkori et al., 2024) uses self-consistency across sampled responses plus conformal prediction to bound the hallucination rate with theoretical guarantees while controlling abstention rate. Gupta (2026) adds a practical warning: isotonic recalibration lowered ECE without improving discrimination (one GPT-4o-mini configuration kept a single confidence value); report correctness-AUROC and risk–coverage, not ECE alone, when choosing a defer threshold.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Clarification should be targeted, not reflexive.** CLARINET (Chi et al., 2024) chooses questions whose answers maximise certainty in the correct candidate (17% over information-gain heuristics, 39% over vanilla prompting on retrieval). STYLE (Chen et al., Findings ACL 2024) improves domain transfer of clarification strategies by ~10% on four unseen domains. SAGE-Agent / structured uncertainty (Suri et al., 2025/2026) separates *specification uncertainty* from *model uncertainty* over tool parameters and uses Expected Value of Perfect Information, gaining 7–39% coverage while asking 1.5–2.7× fewer questions. A caution: Madge, Purver & Poesio (2025) find humans rarely ask clarifications for referential ambiguity but often for task uncertainty, while LLMs do the reverse — so naive "ask when unsure" heuristics misfire.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Tool/structured-output interaction hazard** (Li, Zhang & Lv, 2026): enabling JSON-Schema constraints and tool calling together caused "Tool Suppression" in multiple open-weight models, because schema constraints compile to grammar token masks making tool-call tokens unreachable. Their mitigation is Transparent Two-Pass Execution (execute tools first, then generate schema-constrained answer). This is relevant to a pluggable `ExtractionEngine` that might otherwise combine tool calls and strict JSON in one pass.

[VERIFIED] **ASR itself hallucinates, so extraction cannot assume the transcript is factual.** Koenecke et al. (2024, CHI) found ~1% of Whisper transcriptions contained entirely hallucinated phrases, with 38% of those including explicit harms; Barański et al. (ICASSP 2025) show non-speech audio induces frequent, repeatable hallucinations and that a post-processing "bag of hallucinations" reduces WER. whisper.cpp's own README warns about transcription hallucination with older macOS versions and documents experimental `--print-colors` confidence coding and Silero-VAD gating.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Human-in-the-loop confirmation is an established pattern**, not an admission of failure: Mahbub et al. (2026) use selective expert review for high-uncertainty cases; Xie et al. (2026, EARS) have sub-agents return an actionable failure state/rationale so a coordinator can request clarification or reroute, improving pass rate 68.5% → 78.9% in a production assistant.

[UNKNOWN] **No retrieved source validates any of these techniques specifically on Telugu text or Telugu ASR output.** The techniques are general; their behaviour on Telugu morphology, code-switching and Anganwadi vocabulary must be measured.

## 7. Privacy, offline and local-first options

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **whisper.cpp** (ggml-org, MIT) is a dependency-free C/C++ Whisper inference port supporting CPU-only, integer quantisation, iOS/Android, WebAssembly, Windows/macOS/Linux, Core ML/ANE, OpenVINO, Vulkan, CUDA and NPU backends. Documented memory: tiny ~273 MB, base ~388 MB, small ~852 MB, medium ~2.1 GB, large ~3.9 GB. It ships an offline iPhone demo, a `whisper-stream` mic example, a local HTTP `whisper-server`, Silero-VAD gating and experimental confidence colour-coding. It is inference-only (no training).

[VERIFIED] **On-device ASR with no raw-audio egress is demonstrated in the Gram Vaani setting.** Chauhan et al. (2026) implement a stream-based continual-adaptation framework where raw audio never leaves the local device, alongside replay/EWC stabilisation. This is Hindi and telephony, but it establishes feasibility for the constraint we care about.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **ONNX Runtime** (Microsoft) is a cross-platform inference accelerator with Python/C++/C#/Java/JS/Mobile APIs, execution providers for CoreML, NNAPI, QNN, DirectML, OpenVINO, WebGPU/WebNN, and documented quantisation/graph optimisations. It is a viable deployment target for converted Whisper/Indic models, but the docs explicitly state the user is responsible for validating model accuracy and suitability.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Vosk** offers mostly Apache-2.0 Kaldi models including a 58 MB Telugu model that runs on phones/RPi; it supports dynamic vocabulary reconfiguration on small models. Its Telugu accuracy (87.9 FLEURS WER, vendor-reported) rules it out as a primary recogniser.

[VERIFIED] **Web Speech API must not be assumed to work offline or in Telugu.** MDN (last modified 2026-11-06) marks `SpeechRecognition` as "Limited availability" and not Baseline, and explicitly notes that in Chrome it uses a **server-based recognition engine** — "your audio is sent to a web service for recognition processing, so it won't work offline." MDN documents newer `processLocally`, `SpeechRecognition.available()` and `install()` hooks.

[UNKNOWN] **Chrome/Telugu support must be verified empirically, not assumed.** No authoritative list of Chrome speech-recognition locales (including whether `te-IN` is accepted) was retrieved. The research-only check is `SpeechRecognition.available(['te-IN'])` at runtime plus a short live recognition trial; treat the browser API as a convenience path, not the primary ASR.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Local-first principles** (Kleppmann, Wiggins, van Hardenberg & McGranaghan, 2019, Onward! 2019, doi:10.1145/3359591.3359737): the local device holds the primary copy, servers hold secondary copies; seven ideals include "network optional," offline work, long-term preservation, privacy by default and user control. The essay names SQLite/JSON as recommended archival formats and discusses CRDTs for multi-device sync. This directly supports the planned SQLite local-first store; multi-device conflict resolution is out of scope for a single-worker prototype.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Trade-offs:** on-device Whisper-class models give privacy and offline operation but require ~0.3–4 GB RAM depending on size, need quantisation for low-end devices, and cannot be updated as easily as a hosted model. Cloud APIs give easier streaming, diarization and custom vocabularies (Azure Custom Speech for `te-IN`; Sarvam keyterms) but send audio off-device and introduce per-minute cost and vendor drift. A hybrid — local recogniser plus optional, explicitly consented cloud fallback — is the honest architecture for a research prototype.

[UNKNOWN] **Differential privacy was searched for but not established as relevant here.** With synthetic-only data and no cross-user analytics in the planned scope, there is no identified release mechanism that would require DP; it is not recommended at prototype stage.

## 8. Recommended bake-off design (research-only, no code)

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 0 — freeze the task before ranking systems.** Vistaar's negative benchmark correlation (−0.26 Common Voice vs IndicTTS) means a single test set cannot select a winner. Define a small Telugu matrix with axes: read vs spontaneous; clean vs 3–30 dB SNR noise (Kathbath-Hard recipe); monologue vs multi-speaker; pure Telugu vs Telugu-English code-mix; entity-dense (digits, amounts, names, scheme terms) vs prose.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 1 — candidates.** (a) Whisper-large-v3 vanilla as a floor; (b) a Telugu Whisper fine-tune such as `vasista22/whisper-telugu-large-v2`; (c) IndicWhisper Telugu; (d) IndicWav2Vec or IndicASR; (e) Sarvam Saaras `codemix` mode with keyterms; (f) one of Google/Azure; (g) Vosk small-te as a hard offline floor. The first four can run through whisper.cpp/ONNX-style local inference; the last three define cloud baselines.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 2 — metrics beyond WER.** Report WER/CER, **entity hit rate** for Anganwadi-critical fields, script-collapse rate (Menta 2026), language-ID/switch accuracy on code-mix, and calibration/risk–coverage for deferral. For field reporting, a wrong child name or weight is worse than a fluent but incomplete transcript, so domain-weighted error (as in Singh et al. 2026 and Chandrashekar et al. 2026) should be primary.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 3 — extraction pipeline.** Constrain the output with a config-driven JSON Schema / Pydantic model (Outlines or provider structured output), then require each field to carry a supporting transcript span (SafePassage/LMDX grounding), apply rule-based plausibility ranges (e.g. plausible child age/weight), and abstain + ask one targeted clarification using structured uncertainty/EVPI rather than a generic "please repeat". Keep tool execution in a separate pass from schema-constrained generation (Li et al. 2026).

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 4 — human confirmation and feedback.** Show the extracted fields beside the transcript and source audio; require worker confirmation for fields above a risk threshold (Mahbub et al. 2026; EARS). Log corrections locally for later LoRA/LM adaptation, following the raw-audio-never-leaves-device precedent (Chauhan et al. 2026) and the replay-based stabilisation that reduced forgetting.

[PUBLISHED EVIDENCE] **Stage 5 — synthetic corpus discipline.** Generate code-mixed, entity-dense prompts with BPCC/IndicTrans2 and IndicXlit, synthesise with a measured TTS system, hold out by synthesis system, and always include a small **native-human-recorded sanity set** — Menta (2026) shows synthetic gains can transfer (EHR 0.473 synth vs 0.516 native) but also that gains are entirely corpus-driven (EDSA-isolation ablation EHR 0.020). Check read-prose regression (FLEURS-Te) explicitly.

## 10. Unknowns requiring experiments

[UNKNOWN] **Telugu Anganwadi acoustics.** No corpus or study covers handheld/recording conditions, background chatter, or child/adult mixed speech in anganwadi settings. Measure SNR distributions and WER by recording condition before choosing a model.

[UNKNOWN] **Telugu regional accents.** Rayalaseema vs Coastal Andhra (and other) accent effects on each candidate ASR are unmeasured. IndicVoices supplies district metadata; stratify evaluation by district rather than assuming uniform Telugu performance.

[UNKNOWN] **Spontaneous Telugu–English code-mixed ASR.** No documented system is known to handle it well in field conditions. Construct a code-mix test set with real users and report EHR plus WER; do not infer capability from Sarvam's `codemix` mode or from CoSTA translation results.

[UNKNOWN] **Chrome/Web Speech `te-IN` support.** Must be checked at runtime with `SpeechRecognition.available(['te-IN'])` and a live trial; MDN only establishes that Chrome is server-based and the feature is not Baseline.

[UNKNOWN] **Current commercial pricing for Google STT, Azure Speech and Amazon Transcribe.** Only Sarvam's current prices and the 2023 Vistaar figures were retrieved. Re-quote before any cost comparison.

[UNKNOWN] **Licences for IndicVoices, Shrutilipi, Kathbath, Vistaar, Common Voice (current), FLEURS and TeluguST-46.** Not established from the retrieved sources; legal review needed before redistributing derived synthetic data. IndicTrans2/IndicXlit/whisper.cpp/Vosk licences *were* established.

[UNKNOWN] **Whether Whisper/IndicWhisper ggml or ONNX conversions exist and preserve Telugu accuracy.** Feasibility is inferred from whisper.cpp/ONNX Runtime capabilities; no Telugu-specific converted model was verified.

[UNKNOWN] **Calibration quality on Telugu extraction.** Conformal abstention, EVPI clarification and grounding checks are validated on English/other tasks; thresholds must be calibrated on Telugu synthetic + human sanity data, and ECE alone must not be trusted (Gupta 2026).

[UNKNOWN] **Differential privacy relevance.** No candidate release mechanism requiring DP was identified within the synthetic-only, local-first prototype scope; revisit only if individual-level statistics are ever exported.

[UNKNOWN] **Single-benchmark validity for Telugu.** Given Vistaar's negative benchmark correlations and Telugu's absence from Common Voice in that study, any "best ASR for Telugu" claim from one test set must be treated as unverified.
